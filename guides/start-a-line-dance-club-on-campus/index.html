Campus starter kit

Start a line dance club on your campus.

You do not need to be the best dancer at your college. You need a clear purpose, a few interested people, and a welcoming first experience. This guide gives you a practical path from “someone should start this” to a community that meets again.

Begin here

Start with a simple promise

Starting a college line dancing club is partly about dancing and partly about building community. Your job as the founder is not necessarily to teach every step. Your job is to bring the right people, place, instruction, and support together so more students have a comfortable way to try.

A useful starting promise

We are building a welcoming campus community where students can learn line dancing, meet people, and enjoy moving to music. No partner or previous dance experience is required, and beginners are always welcome.

Keep the first version simple. One reliable beginner gathering every week or every other week is enough to prove that the community should exist.

Step one

Learn your campus process

Search your college website for “student organization registration,” “new club application,” or “student activities.” If the path is unclear, email the office that supports student organizations and ask for the current process.

Find out:

  • When new organizations may apply
  • How many founding members and officers are required
  • Whether a faculty or staff adviser is required
  • Whether you need a constitution or officer training
  • How room reservations and event registration work
  • Whether dance is treated as a student club, recreation program, or club sport
  • What waivers, insurance, or risk-management steps apply
  • How outside instructors become approved vendors
  • How the organization may receive and spend money
  • Which accessibility and nondiscrimination language is required

Ask whether you may hold an informal interest meeting while the application is under review. Do not assume another college’s process applies to yours.

Step two

Find a small, dependable founding team

You do not need a crowd before you apply. You need enough reliable people to meet your school’s requirements and share the work. Look among friends, residence halls, campus dance and fitness groups, music programs, recreation classes, cultural organizations, student affinity groups, and people who already attend local dance events.

Ask two separate questions: “Would you attend?” and “Would you help launch this?” Interest and responsibility are not the same commitment.

A simple interest form

  • Name and school email
  • Graduation year
  • Dance experience, including “none”
  • Preferred meeting days and times
  • Interest in organizing, teaching, or assisting
  • Accessibility needs the organizers should consider
  • Permission to receive club updates

Collect only what you need and use a university-approved form when required.

Step three

Make beginner-friendly a real operating choice

A student entering the room may be excited, nervous, or both. Make the experience understandable before the music starts.

  • Explain the session before teaching begins.
  • Teach unfamiliar movements from the beginning.
  • Do not require partners or physical contact.
  • Make participation, rest, and observation acceptable choices.
  • Avoid calling out reluctant or struggling participants.
  • Share the music volume, activity level, venue access, and session length in advance.
  • Ask before filming or photographing participants.
  • Provide a clear photo opt-out process.
  • Offer a private way to request accommodations.
  • Ask instructors what adaptations they can confidently provide.

The goal is not to promise that one event will work identically for everyone. The goal is to remove unnecessary barriers and respond respectfully when students tell you what would help them participate.

Step four

Choose who will teach

Student-led

An experienced student teaches regular meetings. Pair the teacher with someone who welcomes newcomers and helps from another side of the room.

Professional guest

A local instructor leads the kickoff, a special workshop, or recurring sessions. Ask about true beginner experience, rates, travel, sound, insurance, and vendor requirements.

Campus partner

Campus recreation, a dance department, wellness, or residence life may provide instruction, facilities, funding, or promotion.

Hybrid

Hire a professional for the launch and occasional workshops, then let prepared student leaders run practice sessions between visits.

Use Hire Line Dancers to explore instructors near your campus. Confirm experience, group size, styles, equipment, vendor paperwork, accessibility, cancellation terms, and payment directly with the instructor.

Your first two months

An eight-week launch plan

Week 1

Define the idea

Write the club promise, find the registration process, check for similar groups, and open an interest form.

Week 2

Build the founding team

Recruit the required members, identify possible officers, choose a likely meeting rhythm, and begin the adviser search if needed.

Week 3

Prepare the application

Draft the organization description and constitution, confirm responsibilities, complete training, and submit the registration materials.

Week 4

Plan the first meeting

Request a room, choose the teaching model, contact instructors if needed, and confirm sound, accessibility, and event requirements.

Week 5

Create the launch identity

Choose a clear name, prepare the interest announcement and flyer, and make registration simple if capacity is limited.

Week 6

Promote the kickoff

Use approved campus channels, ask partner groups to share, invite people personally, and confirm every operational detail.

Week 7

Host the first meeting

Arrive early, test the room, welcome students individually, teach for complete beginners, and announce the next gathering.

Week 8

Follow up and improve

Thank attendees, send the next date, collect brief feedback, review the plan with your team, and adjust what comes next.

Registration delays may change the timeline. Keep building interest and planning within campus rules while approvals are pending.

Launch night

A practical first meeting

This sample works for a 75-minute gathering. Adjust it with your instructor and campus requirements.

  1. 0:00 to 0:10, arrival and welcome. Check in attendees, introduce the leaders, explain the purpose, and point out water, exits, restrooms, photo policies, and the option to observe.
  2. 0:10 to 0:20, orientation and warm-up. Explain how line dancing works, review a few movements, and let the instructor lead an appropriate warm-up.
  3. 0:20 to 0:40, first beginner dance. Teach in short sections, practice without music, add music gradually, and repeat without rushing.
  4. 0:40 to 0:50, break and introductions. Give people time for water and casual conversation.
  5. 0:50 to 1:10, second dance or review. Choose based on the room’s comfort and energy.
  6. 1:10 to 1:15, closing. Announce the next meeting, share the club’s communication channel, invite feedback, and thank everyone.

The standard for success

A successful first meeting does not require teaching several dances. It helps new people feel that they can return.

Make it sustainable

Build a realistic first-semester budget

Possible expenses include instructor fees and travel, room rental, sound equipment, approved music services, vendor or insurance costs, printing, supplies, accessibility accommodations, and special-event costs.

Possible campus funding sources include student government, student activities grants, recreation, residence life, wellness programs, arts or cultural funds, department co-sponsorships, and approved fundraising.

Try to keep regular participation free or inexpensive when possible. If you charge dues, explain what they support and ask the school about fee waivers or other ways to participate.

Fill the room

Promote the feeling, not only the dance style

A student who already loves line dancing understands the appeal. A new student needs to know what walking into the room will feel like.

  • Beginners are welcome.
  • No partner is required.
  • No previous dance experience is needed.
  • Steps will be taught from the beginning.
  • Students may come alone or bring friends.
  • Observing and taking breaks are welcome.
  • The time, location, accessibility information, and expected activity level are clear.

Use the official student organization directory, campus event calendar, residence hall channels, approved bulletin boards, orientation fairs, department newsletters, recreation and wellness channels, social media, partner organizations, and personal invitations. Follow campus rules before using class forums, distribution lists, or flyers.

After the kickoff

Turn first-time attendees into returning members

  • Meet at a consistent day and time.
  • Announce future dates before students leave.
  • Greet first-time attendees personally.
  • Begin each meeting with a genuine beginner reset.
  • Repeat popular dances across several meetings.
  • Give members time to talk before or after dancing.
  • Ask which dances, music, and formats they want.
  • Create small volunteer jobs for people who want to help.
  • Invite experienced members to assist without taking over.
  • Develop future officers before current leaders graduate.

The strongest club culture tells a new student, “You can start here,” every week.

Officer roles that match the work

Club lead: purpose, adviser communication, officer meetings, and long-term planning.

Operations lead: rooms, equipment, event checklists, and schedules.

Treasurer: budgets, funding requests, approved purchasing, and financial records.

Programming lead: lessons, workshops, instructors, and member feedback.

Communications lead: promotion, reminders, interest lists, and new-member welcome.

Member experience lead: accessibility information, photo consent, private feedback, and newcomer support.

Common questions

What new founders usually ask

Do I need to be an advanced dancer to start the club?

No. A founder can organize the community while another student or professional leads instruction. Be honest about your experience and bring in qualified help when you need it.

Does the music have to be country?

No. Line dances exist across several music styles. Choose music that suits your community, and confirm what a prospective instructor is comfortable teaching.

Do we need a professional instructor at every meeting?

Not necessarily. Some clubs use student instruction, some hire professionals, and some combine a professional launch workshop with student-led practice between workshops.

Can we meet before the organization is officially approved?

Ask your student activities office. Colleges differ in what an unregistered group may call itself, reserve, promote, or collect money for.

What if we cannot afford an instructor yet?

Explore campus recreation partnerships, department co-sponsorships, student government funding, or one funded launch workshop followed by student-led practice. Confirm the approved payment process before making a commitment.

Take the first step

Your campus community can start with one message.

Check the student organization process. Send the interest note to a few people. Ask one potential adviser for a conversation. Then take the next step.

A line dancing community begins when someone decides there should be a welcoming place for people to try. That person can be you.
